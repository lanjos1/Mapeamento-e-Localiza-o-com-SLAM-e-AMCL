# Mapping and Localization with SLAM + AMCL

## Base

This activity uses the **[lar-deeufba/lar_gazebo](https://github.com/lar-deeufba/lar_gazebo)** repository as infrastructure, developed by the Automation and Robotics Laboratory (LAR) at DEE/UFBA. This repository provides:

- The **3D model of the LAR lab** for simulation in Gazebo
- The **Husky robot** configured for the environment
- The **Docker container scripts** (`build.sh`, `run_husky.sh`, `shell.sh`) that encapsulate the entire ROS Noetic environment

---

## Objective

Generate maps of the simulation environment using two SLAM algorithms (**GMapping** and **Hector SLAM**), run **AMCL** on each generated map, and compare the estimated pose with the ground truth provided by Gazebo.

---

## Part 1 — Map Generation

### 1.1 Record the sensor bag (if you don't already have one)

Open 3 terminals:


## Results and Discussion

### SLAM + AMCL

| Metric                         | Hector SLAM | GMapping |
|---------------------------------|-------------|----------|
| Mean Position Error (m)         | 0.7530 m    | 0.6388 m |
| Position RMSE (m)               | 0.8395 m    | 0.7075 m |
| Final Position Error (m)        | 1.2799 m    | 1.0414 m |
| Mean Orientation Error (°)      | 2.5485°     | 3.1369°  |
| Orientation RMSE (°)             | 3.1337°     | 3.9505°  |
| Stability (Standard Deviation)  | 0.3711°     | 0.3040°  |

### Qualitative Map Analysis

#### Map completeness

Both maps show good completeness — the lab walls are well closed off across almost the entire extent, and the internal sub-regions, the side corridor and the lower alcove, appear fully explored. However, the **Hector SLAM** map leaves a visible region of uncertainty at the entrance (upper-left corner), where the LIDAR performed few overlapping scans.

#### Presence of distortions

GMapping shows a distortion in the upper-left corner, where the LIDAR beam extrapolated beyond the lab's real boundaries, producing a fan-shaped artifact. Hector SLAM has a slight curvature along the long walls, resulting from the absence of odometry to anchor the global alignment between scans.

#### Misaligned walls

In GMapping the walls are well aligned. In Hector SLAM there is a more visible misalignment on the top wall — two consecutive scans produced parallel traces with an offset, indicating that scan matching accumulated a rotation error without odometric correction.

#### False obstacles

Both maps have isolated points in free space, corresponding to objects such as tables, chairs, and computers from the Gazebo 3D model. GMapping filters these points out better through particle weighting, while Hector SLAM retains more points due to consistency between scans, resulting in more points clustered together representing a single object.

#### Unknown regions (gray)

The gray regions are more extensive in the Hector SLAM map, especially at the boundaries outside the lab, since the algorithm does not expand occupancy beyond the LIDAR's effective scans. In GMapping, propagation via particles marginally fills in these boundaries, resulting in fewer gray pixels around them.

#### Localization quality with AMCL

AMCL achieved better positional performance on the GMapping map (RMSE 0.707 m vs. 0.839 m), due to the map's better geometric consistency. On the Hector SLAM map, AMCL compensated for the positional error with a better orientation estimate (RMSE 3.13° vs. 3.95°), leveraging the sharper angular edges produced by pure scan matching. In both cases the final position error is higher than the mean error, showing accumulated drift over the trajectory.

### Critical Analysis

GMapping uses a Rao-Blackwellized particle filter that integrates odometry data during map construction. Since Gazebo provides low-noise odometry, the generated map shows better geometric consistency and better AMCL localization.

Hector SLAM ignores odometry and relies on high-frequency LIDAR scan matching, which makes it more accurate at detecting rotations, producing sharper angular edges on the map and thereby allowing AMCL to estimate the Yaw angle with lower error.

In both algorithms, the Final Position Error is greater than the Mean Error. This behavior is expected in the literature because small uncertainties accumulate over time, generating drift that grows with the distance traveled.
